SLI, SLO & SLA — how reliability actually works

Three letters people mix up constantly. Here's what each one is, how they stack, and a complete worked example on a real service — the Payments API that's live in your demo data right now.

The three terms

One measurement, one target, one promise

They describe the same thing at three levels: what you measure, what you aim for, and what you promise.

SLI — Indicator
What's happening

A number you measure: good ÷ valid events. e.g. "% of Payments requests that were fast & successful." Pure fact, no opinion.

SLO — Objective
What you aim for

Your internal target on that SLI, over a window. e.g. "99.9% over 28 days." The goal you hold yourself to — and alert on.

SLA — Agreement
What you promise

The external contract with a customer, with consequences (credits/penalties). Deliberately looser, e.g. "99.5% or we refund."

SLI the measurement → SLO your target (stricter) → SLA the promise (looser)

99.5% <──────────── 99.9% ────────────> 99.94%
SLA floor SLO target current SLI

The one rule that ties them together

SLA < SLO ≤ SLI (when healthy). You set the SLO stricter than the SLA on purpose, so your own alarm (SLO) trips before the customer contract (SLA) is ever at risk. You react to an internal miss; the customer never sees a breach.

Two more words you need

Error budget & burn rate

  • Error budget = 100% − SLO. If the SLO is 99.9%, you're allowed to fail 0.1% of the time. That 0.1% is a budget you get to spend — on deploys, experiments, the occasional blip.
  • Burn rate = how fast you're spending it, relative to "on pace." 1× = you'll spend exactly the whole budget by the end of the window. 14.4× = the entire 28-day budget gone in ~2 days.

Reliability engineering is really just budget management: spend it deliberately, alert when you're burning it too fast, and freeze risky changes when it runs out.

Complete worked example

The Payments API, end to end

This is the exact service seeded in your ProjectIQ demo. Let's build its reliability story from scratch.

Step 1 — Pick the SLI (what "good" means)

For an availability SLI we count a request as good when it is HTTP 2xx/3xx AND latency < 300 ms. The SLI is then:

SLI (availability)good requests ÷ valid requests

Step 2 — Set the SLO (the target + window)

Objective99.9% availability
Windowrolling 28 days
Traffic in window10,000,000 requests

Step 3 — Derive the error budget (the math)

Allowed failure rate = 100% − 99.9%0.1%
Error budget = 0.1% × 10,000,00010,000 failures

So over 28 days you may "spend" up to 10,000 failed requests and still hit the SLO.

Step 4 — Watch it spend (mid-window snapshot)

Two weeks in, monitoring has recorded 6,500 failures so far:

Budget consumed = 6,500 ÷ 10,00065%
Budget remaining35% · 3,500 failures
Current SLI = (10,000,000−6,500) ÷ 10,000,00099.935% meeting

Error budget remaining: 35% — healthy (SLI 99.935% ≥ target 99.9%).

Step 5 — Layer the SLA on top

You promise customers 99.5% availability, with service credits if you miss. Because your SLO (99.9%) is far stricter, the SLI would have to fall a long way — spending 5× the SLO budget — before the 99.5% contract is even threatened. Your internal alarms fire with huge margin to spare.

The clever part

Burn-rate alerts — page on the fire, ticket on the leak

A raw "SLI below 99.9%" alert is useless — by the time a 28-day average dips, you're already deep in trouble, and tiny blips would page you at 3am. Instead you alert on burn rate over two windows at once (the Google-SRE pattern):

AlertConditionBurnAction in ProjectIQ
fast burn~2% of budget in 1 hour≈ 14.4×Pages the on-call engineer via the escalation policy
slow burn~10% of budget in 6 hours≈ 6×Opens a ticket — no 3am page

Why two windows? The long window proves the problem is real (not a 30-second blip); the short window proves it's still happening right now (so a recovered incident auto-clears). Both must exceed the threshold to fire. When budget hits zero, the error-budget policy kicks in: a change-freeze until reliability recovers.

Live scenario

A bad deploy, minute by minute

  1. 16:00 — A config deploy ships. Payments starts returning 503s. The SLI for the current 5-min bucket craters (good/total ≈ 0/300).
  2. 16:05–16:10 — The beat records more bad buckets. Over the last 5 min and the last hour, the error ratio is ~100% vs an allowed 0.1% → burn rate ≫ 14.4×.
  3. 16:10 — fast burn fires. ProjectIQ raises an alert on the Payments API service and pages the on-call engineer (schedule → escalates to the manager if unacked in 15 min).
  4. 16:12 — The engineer declares an incident (SEV2), takes the IC role, and rolls back the deploy.
  5. 16:25 — 5xx stops. New buckets are healthy; the short-window burn drops below threshold → the burn alert auto-clears.
  6. Aftermath — Those 25 minutes cost a chunk of budget (say it jumps to 78% consumed). Still under 100%, so no freeze — but a retrospective + action items are logged. The customer SLA (99.5%) was never breached, because the SLO alarm fired with margin to spare.

One-line summary

SLI is the thermometer, SLO is the temperature you promise yourself, SLA is the temperature you promise the customer — and the error budget is how much fever you can run before you stop shipping and start fixing.
