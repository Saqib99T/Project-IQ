SLI / SLO / SLA & RCA Governance

A plain-English cheat sheet — read this before the meeting, not during it.
The 30-second version: we measure real performance (SLI), compare it to an internal target (SLO), and separately promise the customer a service level with consequences (SLA). Missing the target eats into an error budget — an early warning, not a surprise. When something serious breaks, the system requires a Root Cause Analysis before anyone can call it closed — evidence attached, a named person signs off.

01 The four terms — one analogy, pizza delivery

SLI Service Level Indicator — the measurement
Plain English: the real number you measure. "Of our last 1,000 deliveries, how many arrived within 30 minutes?"
In Project IQ: every ticket has a live response-time and resolution-time clock, tracked automatically.
We're not guessing how we're doing — every ticket has a running clock, and that clock is the SLI.
SLO Service Level Objective — our internal target
Plain English: the goal we set for ourselves. "We aim for 99% of deliveries within 30 minutes." No contract yet — just our own bar.
In Project IQ: an admin defines it directly — e.g. "99.9% of P1 incidents resolved within 4 hours, measured over 28 days."
The SLO is our own bar — we can raise it, lower it, or tune it without touching a customer contract.
SLA Service Level Agreement — the customer promise
Plain English: the signed commitment with a consequence attached. "If fewer than 95% arrive on time this month, you get a credit."
In Project IQ: every ticket carries a due-by time based on its priority and the customer's contract; breach status is tracked per ticket, not just in aggregate.
The SLA is the one line the customer actually reads — everything else exists to make sure we never surprise them with it.
Error Budget how much "wrong" we're allowed
Plain English: if the SLO is 99%, the other 1% is the budget — an allowance of acceptable misses before we're actually in trouble.
In Project IQ: shown as a live gauge with burn-rate alerts — a fast burn pages someone immediately, a slow burn just opens a ticket.
We don't wait for the breach to react — we watch the budget burn down and get ahead of it.
Bonus terms OLA & UC — same idea, different direction
OLA (Operational Level Agreement): the same promise, but between internal teams — e.g. the network team promises the app team 99.95% uptime.
UC (Underpinning Contract): the same promise, but from an outside vendor to us — e.g. our cloud provider's own uptime guarantee.

02 RCA Governance — the short version

What it is: after a serious incident, the system requires a written Root Cause Analysis before the incident can be closed. Not a suggestion, not an honor system.

Why it exists: under pressure, root-cause reviews are the first thing teams skip. This makes the review automatic and unskippable.

  1. Policy decides who needs one. Rules like "any Sev1" or "any SLA breach on a high-priority ticket" auto-require an RCA — set once, applied every time, no one has to remember.
  2. Evidence is required before approval. A checklist — what happened, the root cause, the fix — has to be filled in, with supporting call/chat recordings attached when relevant.
  3. A named person signs off. An approver (team lead or manager) approves it — timestamped and auditable, not an anonymous checkbox.
This isn't a text box someone might forget to fill in — it's a workflow. The system decides when an RCA is required, blocks closing the incident until the evidence is there, and someone has to put their name on it.

03 How it actually flows in Project IQ, step by step

Three concrete walk-throughs — read these almost verbatim while you click through the matching screen.

A) A ticket's SLA clock, start to finish
  1. Ticket is created. Its priority and product automatically match it to an SLA/OLA/UC policy — no one picks this manually.
  2. The coverage-window clock starts — 24×7 or business-hours only, whichever the matched policy specifies.
  3. At 50% / 75% / 90% of the allowed time, the system raises an internal warning — before anything is actually breached.
  4. The AI predictor separately flags tickets it thinks are trending toward a breach, even earlier than the 90% mark.
  5. If time runs out, the ticket is marked breached — visible on the ticket itself and rolled into SLA compliance reporting.
B) An SLO's error budget, start to finish
  1. An admin defines an SLO — e.g. "99.9% of P1 incidents resolved within 4 hours, over a rolling 28-day window."
  2. Every qualifying incident's real resolution time (the SLI) feeds into that rolling window automatically as it happens.
  3. The gap between target and actual performance is the error budget — shown live as a gauge, not buried in a report.
  4. Burn-rate alerting watches how fast it's spent: burning fast pages the on-call engineer right now; burning slow just opens a ticket for later.
  5. The budget resets as the window rolls forward — one bad week recovers over time, it isn't a permanent black mark.
C) An incident that requires an RCA, start to finish
  1. An incident happens and gets worked like any other — major ones get IC / comms / ops / scribe roles and a tracked timeline.
  2. RCA policy is checked automatically against the incident — does it match a rule (e.g. "Sev1", "SLA breach on high/critical ticket")? Rules are evaluated by priority, first match wins.
  3. If matched, an RCA is auto-created — owner, approver and a due date are pre-assigned by the policy, not chosen by hand.
  4. The owner fills the evidence checklist — what happened, the root cause, the corrective actions — and can attach a support recording if one exists.
  5. If a recording is attached, consent and sensitivity flags must be set before its AI summary can even be viewed; every view or download writes to an access log.
  6. Each corrective action needs to be owner-verified done, or explicitly waived, before the RCA can move to approval.
  7. The named approver signs off — the incident cannot close until this happens. Timestamped, auditable, done.

04 If someone in the meeting asks…

"Isn't this just extra paperwork?"
It only fires for incidents that actually matter — a routine ticket never triggers one. It replaces an informal "lessons learned" email nobody reads with one tracked, provable record.
"What if our target is unrealistic?"
That's exactly why SLO and SLA are separate — we can tune our internal target without renegotiating the customer contract.
"What actually happens when we miss it?"
Nothing happens to the customer automatically — internally, we get an early warning at 50/75/90% of the error budget, so the team reacts before a real breach, not after.
"Who has to approve the RCA?"
Whoever the policy names as approver for that incident type — usually a team lead or manager, not the person who caused or fixed the incident.
